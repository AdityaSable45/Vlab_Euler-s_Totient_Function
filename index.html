Euler's Totient Function

Aim & Objectives

Aim: To implement Euler's Totient function φ(n)

Objectives:

  • To evaluate it for various values of n.
  • Understand what φ(n) counts and why it matters in cryptography.
  • Compute φ(n) directly, by counting coprimes, and verify it against the product formula.
  • Observe how φ(n) behaves for primes, prime powers, and composite numbers.
  • Connect φ(n) to RSA key generation, where it determines the size of the usable exponent space.

Theoretical Concepts

Definition

Euler's totient function φ(n), for a positive integer n, counts how many integers in the range 1 to n are coprime to n — that is, share no common factor with n other than 1.

φ(n) = |{ k : 1 ≤ k ≤ n, gcd(k, n) = 1 }|

Product formula

If the prime factorisation of n is n = p₁a₁ · p₂a₂ ··· pra₁, φ(n) can be computed without listing every coprime:

φ(n) = n · ∏ (1 − 1/pi) for each distinct prime pi dividing n

Key properties

CaseResultWhy
n = 1φ(1) = 1By convention.
n = p (prime)φ(p) = p − 1Every integer below a prime is coprime to it.
n = pkφ(pk) = pk − pk−1Only multiples of p are excluded.
n = a · b, gcd(a,b) = 1φ(n) = φ(a) · φ(b)φ is multiplicative over coprime factors.

Applications in Cryptography:

In RSA, once two primes p and q are chosen and n = p·q, the totient φ(n) = (p−1)(q−1) gives the size of the group used for key generation. The public exponent e is chosen coprime to φ(n), and the private exponent d is e's modular inverse mod φ(n). A weak or predictable φ(n) directly weakens the keypair, which is why n must be the product of two large, well-chosen primes.

Step-by-Step Procedure

  1. Pick a value of n. Enter any positive integer in the simulation panel — there's no upper limit, though very large n will skip the brute-force listing for performance (see note below).
  2. Factorise n. The lab reduces n to its prime factors and their powers.
  3. Apply the product formula. Watch each factor (1 − 1/p) get multiplied in, step by step.
  4. Cross-check by brute force. The lab also lists every k from 1 to n with gcd(k, n) = 1, so you can confirm the count matches the formula's result.
  5. Repeat for a prime, a prime power, and a composite n to see how the three cases from the theory section play out numerically.
  6. Attempt the quiz to confirm your understanding before submission.
